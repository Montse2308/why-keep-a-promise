What you just did at the table follows the experiment of Vanberg (2008). There, the one who decides
is the dictator and the other is the recipient; the chat before the roles was 2 messages each, of at
most 90 characters; and in the partner-switch treatment, 192 people took part, over 8 rounds.

The table simplifies two things. At the table you already know you decide; in the experiment, no
one knew yet while chatting. And when the table switches your partner, it always gives you the same
case: your new partner received a promise from another dictator. That is a fixed example, not a
draw, and it is the case the comparison below uses.

In the rounds where the dictator had promised, the die was rolled 73% of the time with the same
partner, and 54% of the time with a new partner who had been promised by another dictator.

Recipients who had received a promise guessed what the dictator would do, on a five-point scale
from "certainly rolls" to "certainly doesn't roll". Read from 0 to 100, their average guess was 70
without a switch and 68 with one.

Vanberg (2008) concludes that the effect of promises is not explained by changes in what the other
person expects, and that it suggests a preference for keeping one's word in itself.
