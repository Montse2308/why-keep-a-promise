What you just did at the table follows the experiment of Vanberg (2008), step by step.

1. Before anyone knows their role, the two people in each pair chat: 2 messages each, of at most
   90 characters. That is where promises are made.
2. The roles are drawn. One person becomes the dictator, the one who decides; the other becomes
   the recipient, who only receives.
3. In half of the pairs, after the roles are drawn, the dictator gets a new partner. Only the
   dictator learns of the switch.
4. The dictator decides whether to roll the die.

In the partner-switch treatment, 192 people took part, over 8 rounds.

The table simplifies two things. There, you already know you are the one who decides; in the
experiment, no one knew yet while chatting. And when the table switches your partner, it always
gives you the same case: your new partner received a promise from another dictator. That is a
fixed example, not a draw, and it is the case the comparison below uses.

In the rounds where the dictator had promised, the die was rolled 73% of the time with the same
partner, and 54% of the time with a new partner who had been promised by another dictator.

Recipients who had received a promise guessed what the dictator would do, on a five-point scale
from "certainly rolls" to "certainly doesn't roll". Read from 0 to 100, their average guess was 70
without a switch and 68 with one.

Vanberg (2008) concludes that the effect of promises is not explained by changes in what the other
person expects, and that it suggests a preference for keeping one's word in itself.
