## The whole design

The instructions and screens that Vanberg (2008) published fill in what the story leaves out.

- The experiment has 8 rounds. In each one you are matched with another participant, chosen at
  random, and never with the same one twice.
- At the end, one of the 8 rounds is drawn at random, and only that one is paid.
- Roles are drawn again in every round, with the same chance of getting either.
- In the chat, messages alternate, and a draw decides who writes first.
- A dictator whose partner was switched can read both chats: their own, and the one their new
  partner had with someone else.
- At the end of each round, the recipient only sees their own payoff.
- The guesses were paid, in the rounds not drawn for the decision. The recipient picked one of
  five answers, from "certainly rolls" to "certainly doesn't roll", and earned 65, 60, 50, 35 or 15
  cents depending on the answer and on what the dictator did: 65 for a sure answer that turned out
  right, 15 for one that turned out wrong. The dictator, in turn, guessed the recipient's answer and
  earned 50 cents for getting it right.

## Every cell of the switch treatment

The story compares two of the cells. The treatment has six: whether the dictator promised, crossed
with whom they faced: the same partner, a new partner who had been promised by another dictator, or
a new partner whom no one had promised anything. Each count is of decisions, one per dictator and
round, written as it is in the data, over the cell's total.

The last column is the dictators' second-order belief: what a dictator believes their partner
expects of them, on the same five-point scale, read from 0 to 100.

## Vanberg's argument

Vanberg (2008) reads the table through one comparison. Among dictators who had promised, rolling
falls from 73% with the same partner to 54% with a new partner who had been promised by someone
else. What those dictators believed their partner expected hardly moves: 80 against 76. Behaviour
changes where beliefs do not, so a change in beliefs cannot be what makes promises work.

## The baseline treatments

Vanberg also ran two simpler treatments, reported in his Appendix A: the same game with the chat
and without it, with no partner switch. With the chat, the die was rolled in 92 of 128 decisions;
without it, in 67 of 128. Each treatment had 32 people, over 8 rounds.

## Counted again, in fractions

The counts on this page were not copied from the article. Each was counted again from the data
Vanberg published with it, `switch.dat` and `baseline.dat`, following his own program,
`promises.do`. The page keeps every count as a fraction of whole numbers and rounds only when it
writes a rate out of 100: to the nearest whole number, halves up, in integer arithmetic, so no step
adds an error of its own. A test checks that the six cells hold every decision of the treatment:
half of its 192 people decide in each of its 8 rounds.
