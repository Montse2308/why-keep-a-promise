### A third reason, in between.

Vanberg's design separates what the other person expects from your word. But there is a third
reason in between: it hurts you to let an expectation down, but only if you created it. This is
personal guilt, from Kawagoe and Narita (2014).

<!-- beat:alike -->

### A lab cannot tell it apart.

With a partner switch, you did not create your new partner's expectation, so personal guilt also
makes you roll less. And in any one-round experiment, personal guilt and partner-specific
commitment, your word given to that person, predict the same thing. That is why Vanberg's result
fits both.

<!-- beat:trust -->

### What separates them is background trust.

It is what people expect of someone before any promise is made. In the specification of Kawagoe and
Narita (2014), personal guilt is the product of two amounts: the expectation that was already
there, and what your promise added to it.

<!-- beat:middle -->

### It weighs most in the middle.

If no one expected anything beforehand, the product is zero. If everyone already expected it, your
promise added nothing, and the product is zero too. So it weighs most in the middle, at {peak}.
Your word to that person weighs the same in any world.

Each world has its background trust held fixed: this is not a population moving along the curve.

lab session sees a single point of the curve. Tracing it takes comparing populations with different
background trust.
